### 1 · The quote
*Line 2 is the DEA quote. Read it flat and neutral, as a quotation. The screen types it out as you speak.*

> There's a page on the website of the Drug Enforcement Administration Museum, an institution of the United States federal government, and it says this.

> The earliest reference to opium growth and use is in 3400 B.C. when the opium poppy was cultivated in lower Mesopotamia. The Sumerians referred to it as Hul Gil, the 'joy plant.'

### 2 · The sentence everywhere
*Build pace a little through the list. Land "two words."*

> You have almost certainly met that sentence. It's in hundreds of books. It's been on the PBS Frontline website for a quarter of a century. It opens more histories of opium than any other fact, because it's a wonderful way to begin: the oldest drug, the oldest civilisation, and a name so perfect it feels like the whole subject in two words.

### 7 · One: the date
*Say the number as a heading: "One." (beat) "The date is before writing." The giant 3400 B.C. gets struck on screen as you finish that line.*

> One. The date is before writing.

> Proto-cuneiform, among the earliest writing anywhere, begins somewhere around 3350 to 3300 BC. In 3400 BC there is nothing to write a word like hul gil in. In Mesopotamia, nobody was writing anything down yet.

---

### 8 · Two: the language
*Steady, explanatory.*

> Two. The language can't be identified.

> When writing does appear, the earliest tablets are administrative: goods in, goods out of institutional stores. They aren't trying to write down speech, and they contain almost no evidence of what language their writers spoke. Sumerian is only securely recorded from around 3000 to 2900 BC.

> So the claim gives a phrase to a people whose language, at that date, can't be read off the tablets at all.

---

### 9 · Three: the signs
*Say ḪÚL as "khool" (throaty h, like "loch") and GÍL as "gil".*

> Three. The signs don't mean "joy plant."

> The reading rests on two signs. The first, ḪÚL, can carry a sense of rejoicing. But the second, GÍL, doesn't mean plant. It means something closer to stinking, or foul.

> The Sumerian marker that actually tells you a word is a plant is a different sign entirely. The reading Assyriologists accept now is a compound meaning, roughly, a bitter or foul cucurbit.

---

### 10 · Four: the plant
*Citrullus colocynthis = "sit-RULL-us kol-oh-SIN-this". Böck = "berk".*

> Four. The plant is probably a bitter gourd.

> Most likely it's colocynth, Citrullus colocynthis, the bitter apple. It's a desert gourd, botanically a cousin of the watermelon, that was used as a violent purgative.

> Barbara Böck, whose 2023 herbal with Kew is the current standard reference on Mesopotamian plants, says the opium poppy is simply absent from the cuneiform record, or else hidden behind one of the many plant names nobody has been able to identify.

---

*The payoff. Slower than the first time, with a smile in the voice.*
